# A concurrency limiter bounds the requests running, queues a few for a while in arrival order, and answers the rest 503 with Retry-After

`NewConcurrencyLimiter(ConcurrencyOptions{Name, Limit, Backlog, MaxWait, NoBacklog})` returns a `ConcurrencyLimiter`. Its `Middleware()`, given with `Use` to the app, a group or a route, lets at most `Limit` requests run at once behind it. When `Limit` are running, the next `Backlog` requests wait for a slot in arrival order for at most `MaxWait`, and every other request, and every request that waited `MaxWait` without a slot, is refused with a 503 problem+json carrying `Retry-After` ([ADR 0012](0012-client-errors-are-400-problem-json.md)). Decided and built on 2026-11-17 (`concurrency.go`, `concurrency_test.go`, `concurrency_budget_test.go`) for the roadmap's load-shedding item, whose trigger is a handler too costly to run unbounded, such as a report over a year of data.

```go
reports := bites.NewConcurrencyLimiter(bites.ConcurrencyOptions{Name: "reports", Limit: 4})
app.Group("/reports", bites.Use(reports.Middleware()))
```

**The shape is a middleware made by a constructor, as `RateLimiter` and `Timeout` are, and not a route option.** An agent that has written `bites.Use(bites.Timeout(2*time.Second))` or `limiter.Middleware()` writes this one the same way, and there is one way to give a limit to a scope. The scope is the middleware's: given to `app.Use` it bounds every request the app serves, a 404 included; given to a group with `Use` it bounds the group; given to a route it bounds the route. One limiter given to several scopes is one limit shared by all of them (`g.Group("/admin", Use(l.Middleware()))` is one pool for the group, where `MaxInFlight`'s group option is a count for each route, [ADR 0024](0024-limits-are-on-by-default.md)), and one limiter made for each route is a limit for each: the sharing is the identity of the value the agent holds, not a rule to remember. A route option was rejected for that: it has to choose between a count for each route and a count for the group, and either choice surprises half of the people who read it, and it needs a third spelling for the app (`WithX`) beside the option for groups and routes. A middleware also leaves `routeConfig`, which several changes edit at once, alone.

**Where it sits in the order of [ADR 0048](0048-groups-and-the-order-things-run-in.md).** A group or route middleware runs after the match and before the Providers, the decoding and the body: a request that is refused or is waiting has had its body not read at all, so a client that sent `Expect: 100-continue` has not been told to send it, and one that did not has its bytes held by the kernel's buffers and the sender's own, and no body timer runs ([ADR 0024](0024-limits-are-on-by-default.md) arms it where the body is read). The limit protects the handler's capacity and shares it first come first served, so a RateLimiter by address goes outside it, `limiter.Middleware()` before `reports.Middleware()`, and a request that a sign-in middleware would refuse never takes a place. A Provider, such as `BearerAuth`, runs after every middleware: an unauthenticated client can fill the backlog, which is why the guide says to put the per-client limit outside.

**The options, and what zero means.** `Name` is required, at most 32 letters, digits, `-` or `_`: it is in the 503's detail, in the log line that says the limiter is refusing and in `Stats`, so an operator reading any of them knows which limit it is, and the registration line it is made on is in every refusal of the constructor. `Limit` is required and at least 1. `Backlog` is how many may wait, `Limit` when zero: the queue is never unbounded, because its length is something a client controls ([ADR 0024](0024-limits-are-on-by-default.md)), and `Limit` is a judgement, not a measurement, that holds as many waiting as are running, so a burst of that size is absorbed with a wait of one handler's time and anything beyond is refused at once. `MaxWait` is how long one request may wait, one second when zero: another judgement, unmeasured, for a wait a person does not read as a hang while a queue deeper than that only turns overload into latency, and it is also what `Retry-After` says (below). `NoBacklog` refuses at once when `Limit` are running, with no waiting. It is a field of its own because a zero `Backlog` is the default, as every zero in an options struct is ([ADR 0024](0024-limits-are-on-by-default.md)), and a negative one is refused like every negative bound, so "no queue" needed a spelling that cannot be read as unset or as unlimited.

**Refusals** (rows of `refusal_test.go`; the constructor panics, as `NewRateLimiter` does, naming the constructor, the field and the line it was called from): `Limit` below 1, in the message for zero and for a negative number; `Backlog` or `MaxWait` given with no `Limit` (the message says they only bound the requests waiting for a slot); a `Limit` or a `Backlog` above 2^30, which the packed counts cannot hold; a negative `Backlog`; a negative `MaxWait`; `NoBacklog` with a `Backlog` or a `MaxWait`; a `Name` that is empty, longer than 32 or not letters, digits, `-` and `_`. **What cannot be caught before the first request:** a limiter given to a route whose handler never takes long (it limits nothing it was needed for, and costs one compare-and-swap a request); a `Limit` above what the machine can run (it is the application's number); and a limiter given to two apps, which the guide says not to do (each app's Shutdown tells it to drain, so the first to shut down sends the other's waiting requests away).

**503, and not 429.** A 429 says this client has made too many requests, which is the `RateLimiter`'s statement about a key; a shed request has made no more than anyone else, and the server cannot take it now whoever sent it. 503 Service Unavailable with `Retry-After` is RFC 9110's answer for exactly that, a temporary inability to handle the request, and it is what the proxies in front of an app treat as retryable on another instance (nginx's `proxy_next_upstream http_503`, the `5xx` retry policy of Envoy), where a 429 is returned to the client and read as a signal to slow that client down. `MaxInFlight` of an Upload route already answered 503 with `Retry-After` for the same condition, and [ADR 0012](0012-client-errors-are-400-problem-json.md)'s rule that a limit has its own status holds: this is neither the 400 of a mistake nor the 429 of a rate. `Retry-After` is `MaxWait` rounded up to whole seconds, at least 1, and 1 with `NoBacklog`: by then the requests that were queued when this one was refused have been served or refused. It is deterministic: a client that retries in lockstep with others can synchronise, so the guide tells a client to add jitter, and jitter on the server was left out because Retry-After counts whole seconds and a spread of one or two is a larger change than the advice it gives.

**The queue: arrival order, one lock, no goroutine for a waiter.** A request that finds `running < Limit` and an empty backlog takes a slot with one compare-and-swap on a word that holds the running count (low 32 bits) and the waiting count (high 32 bits) and does no more: it does not touch a list, a timer or a channel, and a slot is given back the same way when nothing waits. A request that must wait takes a mutex, joins a doubly linked list at the tail, and blocks on its own connection's goroutine, the one it already has, in a `select` on a pooled channel of capacity 1 and on the request context's done channel ([ADR 0005](0005-our-own-http1-engine-with-a-goroutine-per-connection.md): a request, a handler and a waiter alike run on the goroutine of their connection). A release with a non-empty backlog does not free the slot: it hands it to the head of the list under the mutex, so a request that arrives a moment after a release cannot pass the waiters, which is what first come first served means here, and a slot is never free while someone waits. Each waiter has a timer, an `AfterFunc` on the app's clock reset in place when the waiter is reused (the same way `Timeout`'s is), so a test moves `bitetest.Clock` instead of waiting; its callback takes the mutex, and if the waiter is still waiting removes it and wakes it as timed out. All three events that end a wait (a slot handed over, the timer, the context's end) are decided under the mutex by the waiter's state, so each waiter ends exactly one way, and a waiter whose timer might still be about to run when its request ended is not put back in the pool, because a late callback would act on whatever request the waiter served next. A single timer for the whole queue was considered, since every wait has the same length and so expires in arrival order, and rejected: it needs the clock at construction, when the app's is not yet known, and a limiter in two apps on two clocks (every test) would not work.

**A request that leaves.** A queued request whose client disconnects, or whose context ends for any other reason (a `Timeout` outside the limiter whose deadline passes), wakes on its done channel, takes its node out of the list and the waiting count, and returns; it takes no slot and holds no place, so the next arrival may use it, and it is counted as `Left`, not as shed: nobody is waiting for the answer, and the access log and the Observers see an abandoned request ([ADR 0016](0016-the-handler-context-is-cancelled-on-disconnect.md), [ADR 0051](0051-a-request-is-observed-when-it-starts-and-when-it-has-really-ended.md)). A slot that is handed over at the very moment the request's context ends is handed on to the next waiter: running a handler for a request nobody waits for would spend a slot on nothing. A handler that panics or returns an error releases its slot, because it is released in a `defer` of the middleware, below the engine's recover ([ADR 0028](0028-panics-are-recovered-by-the-engine.md)). A `Stream` or `SSE` that a handler returns runs after every middleware has returned ([ADR 0030](0030-streaming-is-a-return-type.md)), so the limit bounds the time to produce the stream and not its life, as `Timeout` does; a limit on open streams is the connection limit or a count the application keeps.

**Shutdown.** When `App.Shutdown` reaches the engine's Shutdown, after `WithShutdownDelay` has served for its length, the app tells every limiter that has served one of its requests to drain: the requests waiting are refused at once with a 503 that says the server is shutting down, with `Retry-After`, so a client retries on another instance without waiting out `MaxWait`, and a request that arrives afterwards and would have to wait is refused the same way; requests that hold a slot finish as every request in flight does ([ADR 0029](0029-shutdown-drains-and-then-closes.md)). The drain does not start with readiness failing, since during the delay the app still serves ([ADR 0052](0052-health-and-readiness-follow-shutdown.md)). `Close` ends every connection, and the waiting requests leave through their done channels. A limiter learns of its app at the first request it serves, which also writes one Info line (`bites: concurrency limiter in use`, with the name, limit, backlog and `MaxWait`), so the start-up log does not list a limiter that has served nothing: a middleware is a function value that Registration cannot look inside.

**The framework says when it sheds** ([ADR 0051](0051-a-request-is-observed-when-it-starts-and-when-it-has-really-ended.md), principle 2).

- The answer says which limit and why, in its detail, and carries `Retry-After`: `the "reports" limit of 4 running requests is reached and its backlog of 4 is full; retry in 1 s`, `... and no slot freed within 1s; retry in 1 s`, `the server is shutting down and the "reports" limit is not taking more requests; retry in 1 s`. The errors are made once, when the limiter is made, so a refusal allocates nothing of its own.
- A Warn line, `bites: load shed: a concurrency limit is refusing requests with 503`, with the limiter, the reason, the number refused since the last line, the running and waiting counts, the limit, the backlog, `MaxWait` and the method, path and request id of the request that triggered it. It is written at the first refusal and then at most once a second, on the app's clock, with the count since the last line: a line for every refused request would multiply the load that is being shed, and one a second is enough to see an overload begin and how large it is.
- `errors.Is(out.Err, bites.ErrShed)` in an Observer's `Outcome`, so a tracker and a span can tell a shed 503 from a 503 the handler chose, and `Metrics` counts it in `bites_requests_shed_total{method,route}`, next to the abandoned counter. The access log's `error` attribute is the detail above, so its record says the same.
- A request that waited and then ran adds `limiter` and `limiter_wait` to its access record (`Request.AddLogAttrs`), so a slow request is not read as a slow handler.
- `Stats()` returns the counts for a gauge of the application's own: `Running` and `Waiting` now, and the counters `Queued`, `Waited` with `WaitTotal`, `ShedFull`, `ShedWait`, `ShedShutdown` and `Left`.
- The API document gives every route behind the limiter, or an app that has it, a `503` that refers to `components.responses.ServiceUnavailable`, a problem+json answer with `Retry-After` ([ADR 0069](0069-the-api-document-states-what-a-routes-providers-and-middleware-ask-and-answer-and-a-route-can-be-left-out.md)); a middleware the application wrote around the limiter adds nothing, as for `RateLimiter`.

**The axes** ([ADR 0003](0003-the-trade-budget.md)). Allocations a request, a typed JSON POST behind the limiter with a slot free: **2**, the same as without and the same as behind a middleware that does nothing (`TestATypedJSONPOSTBehindAConcurrencyLimiterWithAFreeSlotStillAllocatesTwice`). A request that waits: **about 3 to 4 more**, measured as 3.4 more than the same two requests with a slot free, mean of 300 cycles in one process (a done channel, the slice of the two log attributes and their boxing, and the slot handoff's wake), with the waiter, its channel and its timer pooled, about 200 B each (a count of the struct, not measured); `TestARequestThatWaitsInAConcurrencyLimitersBacklogCostsALogAttributeAndAChannelMore`, which fails past 6. A request that is refused: **5**, what any 503 returned by a middleware costs, since the error is made beforehand (it was 7 when the error's `Unwrap` made a slice each call; `TestARequestRefusedByAConcurrencyLimiterCostsWhatAnErrorAnswerCosts` holds it equal to a middleware that returns a ready 503). Memory per idle connection: none, nothing is held between requests. Throughput and p99, a typed GET with a no-op handler through one limiter shared by every connection on the in-memory connection, `go test -bench` with the server and the clients sharing a Ryzen 7 9700X (not pinned, so the clients compete with the server), the tree at `1a466f6` plus this change, eight interleaved runs of 1 s: at 16 threads 207 to 225 ns a request with the limiter and 214 to 229 ns with a middleware that does nothing, unchanged inside the spread; at 8 threads 205 to 247 ns and 207 to 362 ns, medians 229 and 219, unchanged inside the spread; at 4 threads 325 to 439 ns and 310 to 430 ns, unchanged. A first version, in which the fast path took a mutex, measured 256 ns at the median against 209 at 8 threads, which is 22% and past the 10% a feature may give up, and 280 against 241 at 16, which is why the counts are one word. A route without the limiter pays nothing: no field of the route or the app is read on the request path. The numbers are one machine in one session; a different core count would move the contended case, which `BenchmarkAConcurrencyLimiterWithAFreeSlot` reruns.

**Scenarios it was put against.**

- _A slow handler and a burst of ten times the limit_ (`TestABurstOfTenTimesTheLimitRunsLimitAtOnceQueuesBacklogAndRefusesTheRest`, `Limit: 4`, 40 requests at once): 4 run, 4 wait, 32 are refused at once with `Retry-After`; the peak of handlers running at once is 4; when the 4 finish, the 4 that waited run, in the order they arrived (`TestRequestsOverTheLimitWaitAndRunInTheOrderTheyArrived`).
- _Clients that disconnect while queued_ (`TestAClientThatDisconnectsWhileQueuedLeavesTheBacklogAndFreesItsPlace`, and 200 clients that leave at times spread around `MaxWait` and the freeing of slots in `TestAManyClientsThatLeaveWhileQueuedNeverLeakASlotOrAPlace`, under `-race`): nothing is left running or waiting, every request that joined the backlog is counted as ending one way, and the peak of handlers stays at the limit.
- _Parallel traffic against a small limit_ (`TestSustainedParallelTrafficNeverRunsMoreThanLimitAndEndsWithNothingHeld`, 24 clients, `Limit: 3`): no more than 3 run at once, the limiter's refusals equal the 503s the clients saw, and nothing is held at the end.
- _Shutdown with requests queued_ (`TestShutdownSendsTheWaitingRequestsAwayAndLetsTheRunningOnesFinish`): the waiting requests are refused at once with the shutting-down detail and `Retry-After`, the running request finishes with a 200, and Shutdown returns after it.
- _A panic in a handler_ (`TestAPanicInAHandlerReleasesItsSlot`): the slot is back with `Limit: 1` and `NoBacklog`, three panics in a row, and the next request runs.
- _A deadline outside the limiter_ (`TestATimeoutOutsideTheLimiterTakesAWaitingRequestOutOfTheBacklog`): a waiting request whose `Timeout` passes leaves the backlog.
- _A hostile client that floods the route_: it fills the running slots and the backlog and the rest of its requests are refused at once, at the cost of a lock-free check and a prepared answer; the legitimate client that arrives then is refused as well, which is what a limit that is not per client does. The answer to that is a `RateLimiter` outside it, which the guide says, and the 503 does not name a client.
- _The slot count and the handlers of another route_: a limiter shared across a group counts the group, so a slow route can starve a quick one in the same group; the group is for routes that should share a pool, and a limiter for each route is the other spelling.

**What it fails at.** The limit is by request count, not by cost: four cheap requests and four expensive ones hold the same slots. It is first come first served and not per client or per priority, so a burst from one client delays everyone and a request that matters waits behind one that does not. A request waits holding its connection and its unread body, so a client that sent the body without `Expect: 100-continue` may find its own write timeout reached before `MaxWait`, and a slow reader's connection counts against the connection limit while it waits. `Retry-After` is the same for every refused client. A `Stream`'s life and a WebSocket's are not counted: the slot is released when the chain returns. A limiter is not listed in the start-up log, since Registration cannot see inside a middleware; it logs itself once at its first request. One limiter on two apps is drained by whichever app shuts down first, including the other's waiting requests. The waiter's timer is a runtime timer, so a burst of N waiters is N timers, which the runtime handles but a very large `Backlog` makes expensive to arm. Timers use the app's clock, and a limiter whose pooled waiters last saw another clock builds a new timer, which is a few allocations once in a test that moves between apps.

**What was rejected.** *A route option `MaxConcurrent(n)` and `WithMaxConcurrent` on the app*: above. *Extending `MaxInFlight`, the Upload option, to every route*: its check is at the head, before `100 Continue`, with the connection closed on refusal so the client never sends the body, and it holds its slot through the whole upload, which is the Upload route's own purpose; a request limiter with a backlog is a different decision, and one name for both would make the group's count (for each route) and the new count (shared by the group) differ under one spelling. `MaxInFlight` stays as it is, for uploads, and the guide says which to use for what: an Upload route may carry both, the first bounding the bodies in flight and the second the handler's work. *429*: above. *A queue with no bound in count or in time*: a queue that grows with a client's rate is the memory and latency the limits of [ADR 0024](0024-limits-are-on-by-default.md) exist to refuse. *A limit with a default that is on for every route*: it would 503 legitimate traffic on a route nobody asked it for, as the reason `MaxInFlight` has no default says. *A goroutine for each waiter, or a worker pool that takes requests off a channel*: the request already has a goroutine, its connection's, and moving it to another costs a goroutine and a copy of what the handler needs for nothing; a buffered channel as the semaphore was the first sketch and loses first come first served (a channel's waiting senders are woken in order, but a request that arrives as a slot is freed can take it from under them) and has no way to take a waiter out of the middle when its client leaves. *Logging every refusal*: above. *Refusing the oldest waiter instead of the newest (LIFO shedding)*: it keeps the latency of the requests that are served low under sustained overload, at the price of a rule that is hard to explain and of refusing a request that has already waited; first come first served with a short bounded wait was chosen as the default an agent can reason about, and a different policy is a different type if an application needs it. *A refusal that closes the connection, as `MaxInFlight` does*: it was right for an upload whose body would otherwise be sent; here the body has not been read, and the engine already closes the connection after the answer when the unread body is chunked, was waited for with `Expect: 100-continue`, or is too large to skip, and skips a small one so the connection carries on; closing every shed connection would add a handshake to each retry of a client that sent a small request.
